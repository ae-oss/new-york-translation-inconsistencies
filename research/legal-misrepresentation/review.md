## PSL-COVID-001: current special-leave claim

**Independent source check.** The active [DOL FARE page](https://dol.ny.gov/nys-hero-act-and-covid-19-regulations-fare-grant) presently says: “NYS COVID-19 Leave benefits and protections are still in effect and have no expiration date.” The surrounding text refers to mandatory or precautionary quarantine/isolation orders and links the special COVID-19 Paid Sick Leave fact sheets. The page displays no publication or last-update date. It was accessed through web retrieval on 2026-10-26 and a browser on 2026-10-27; a dated rendered-HTML and full-page screenshot capture is preserved below.

**Independent binding-law check.** The [signed A8806C / S8306C page](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2023/A8806/amendment/C) records chapter 56, Laws of 2024, signed 2024-04-20. Its final Part M § 1 amends § 2 of chapter 25, Laws of 2020, so that the special act “shall expire and be deemed repealed July 31, 2025.” The [Workers' Compensation Board's current employer/payer page](https://apps.wcb.ny.gov/covid-19/information-employers-payers.jsp) independently states that the COVID-19 quarantine leave law expired on 2025-07-31 and no benefits are available *under that law*. Ordinary sick leave under [Labor Law § 196-b](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/LAB/196-B) continues; § 196-b(4) allows qualifying illness, care, and safe-leave uses, subject to the statute's coverage, accrual, pay, and annual-use rules. The sunset does not by itself decide claims that accrued before 2025-07-31, private contractual benefits, or other independently applicable leave/protection laws.

**Applicability and strongest counterargument.** A state agency could mean that ordinary sick leave remains available when a worker has COVID-19. That reading cannot readily explain “NYS COVID-19 Leave” and “no expiration date” in a paragraph about quarantine/isolation orders, immediately followed by the special-program fact sheets. The surviving § 196-b right is not an unlimited, independent quarantine-leave package. The page's separate promises of job protection, financial compensation, and anti-retaliation should not all be described as categorically false: some protections may exist under § 196-b or other law. Frame this case around the expired *special statutory entitlement* and treat the other benefits as qualifications, not separate unsupported charges.

**Same-facts example.** A covered private-sector worker receives a new COVID isolation order in October 2026 and has already used the ordinary sick leave available under § 196-b. Reading the DOL page, the worker expects additional quarantine leave under the 2020 act. That extra act expired in July 2025; whether another source of leave applies would require separate facts. The potential harm is planning around a nonexistent extra statutory benefit. No real worker, loss, agency denial, or reliance is documented.

**Historical and correction status.** The statement became inaccurate by the 2025 sunset if it remained displayed. Without a posting or revision date, do not claim it was false when first published. The WCB page contains an accurate correction, but the captured DOL FARE page does not. The DOL HERO overview and the 2022 paid-sick-leave FAQ repeat parts of this theme with more ambiguous or visibly older context; treat them as related exposure, not extra findings.

## UI-01 and UI-02: claimant hearing requests

**Independent source check.** The current [DOL Unemployment Insurance Contact page](https://dol.ny.gov/node/206), “Request a Hearing,” says: “Your written request must be received within 30 days of the determination.” It then gives the DOL mailing address. No date is displayed. The [DOL Top FAQs](https://dol.ny.gov/unemployment-insurance-top-frequently-asked-questions), question 15, separately says the online or mail request “must be made or postmarked no later than 30 days from the date of the determination notice.” Both were verified on active agency pages and captured as HTML; a full-page screenshot of UI-01 is preserved below.

**Independent binding-law check.** [Labor Law § 620(1)(a)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/LAB/620) allows a claimant to seek a hearing within 30 days after mailing or personal delivery of the initial determination and permits a physical/mental-incapacity extension. The Appeal Board's published [12 NYCRR § 461.1(a)](https://uiappeals.ny.gov/appeal-board-rules-0) expressly treats a request postmarked within 30 days of the party's *receipt* of the determination as timely, also recognizing specified proof of fax/electronic/personal submission. Absent contrary proof, receipt is presumed no later than five business days after the determination's stated mailing date. The Board's [May 7, 2026 decision 640066](https://uiappeals.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/05/640066-appeal-decision.pdf), p. 2, applied the five-business-day receipt presumption and counted 30 calendar days from that date. The [current DOL Hearing Process FAQ](https://dol.ny.gov/hearing-process-frequently-asked-questions) accurately describes postmark or other proof and the receipt presumption. The source PDF of the rule is marked as compiled through 2019/revised 2020, so a final publication review should still check the most recent official rule/register for intervening amendments. Recent agency practice strongly corroborates continuing applicability.

**Applicability and strongest counterargument.** UI-01 could be intended as a conservative mailing recommendation, or “received” could describe online submission. Its section supplies a physical mailing address and uses “must”; it presents agency receipt as mandatory, not as a recommendation. Another DOL page's correct wording does not cure the contact page for a reader who relies on it alone. UI-02 correctly recognizes postmark but potentially shortens the start date to the printed notice date; unlike UI-05's permissive general summary, its “must ... no later” wording is categorical. Both are part of one deadline issue, not two independently counted misrepresentations. The specific notice sent to a claimant may contain more accurate instructions, which could reduce exposure or harm; no notice exemplar or reliance case has been verified here.

**Same-facts example.** A determination is mailed on day 0 and received five business days later. The claimant mails a signed hearing request with a postmark 29 calendar days after receipt, but DOL receives the letter four days later. Section 461.1(a) treats the postmark as timely, subject to any contrary evidence about receipt and filing; the Contact page's “received within 30 days” wording tells the claimant to believe the request is too late. The Top FAQ's printed-date wording may independently deter that same timely filing. This is a legally supported potential consequence, not documented actual harm.

| UI-02 | **Escalated from negative** | Top FAQ says “must” postmark by 30 days from notice date; § 461.1(a) permits 30 days from receipt. Include as the same UI deadline cluster, with no second finding count. |
